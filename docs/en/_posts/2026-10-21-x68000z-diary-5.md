[Previously]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url en/2026-10-21-x68000z-diary-4 %}): TCP/IP over Wi-Fi was running at 68kbps. I told the AI (Claude) "let it run overnight" and went to bed. That was a mistake. In this series, the AI does almost all of the code and research. My jobs are swapping the Pico and pressing the power button.

## Morning. It's dead

I woke up to a report from the AI. The Z had stopped responding. Silence on rcmd. Silence on ping.

Okay. Let's stay calm. Here are the facts the AI lined up for me overnight:

- It died about 22 minutes after the test started.
- Power-cycling the Pico fixes it.
- slipd.x's counters show the exchange count stuck at **65,536**, with only retries and errors still going up.

65,536. No programmer looks at that number and thinks "coincidence."

The read window for responses (the range we read replies from, shifting 8 sectors each time and wrapping back to the start at the end) is 0x80000 sectors, and each exchange uses 8 of them. 0x80000 ÷ 8 = 65,536. So it died exactly when it had gone once around the read window. 65,536 exchanges at one every 20ms is 22 minutes. The math checks out.

The culprit is the Z's read cache. Once we wrap around to the same LBA, the Z's Linux doesn't bother asking the Pico. It just returns the response it remembers from 22 minutes ago. The sequence number doesn't match, so slipd.x throws it away and reads again, but everywhere in the read window is already cached. We're stuck.

Power-cycling the Pico fixes it because, from the Z's point of view, the USB disk disappears entirely, and the cache gets thrown out along with it. The Pico was innocent. Sorry I doubted you.

## A bigger read window won't boot

The AI's fix was simple. Make the read window bigger, so old entries get pushed out of the cache before we come back around. Make ID6 nearly 4GB, and use 2GB of it as the read window. I pulled the Pico, plugged it into the Mac, had it flashed and put it back in the Z.

The Z's answer: "Can't boot from disk."

Oh. Great.

We went back to 2GB and fit the read window inside it. Now it boots, but hangs when CONFIG.SYS loads the B: driver. Some digging revealed that the B: driver (scsiremote.sys) reads its responses from 0x200000 to 0x3fffff. The AI's read window overlapped that, so netpipe was stealing responses meant for the driver. From the driver's point of view, the reply never comes. Of course it hangs.

At this point the AI and I both got a little confused. I swapped the Pico four times. We moved the window somewhere that didn't overlap, and got "Can't boot from disk" again. But then I power-cycled with the same firmware, and it booted just fine.

It looks like "Can't boot from disk" has nothing to do with LBAs. It's a race at power-on: does the Pico finish connecting to Wi-Fi and SMB in time or not? The failure at 4GB might have been that too. I never checked. Lesson relearned: change one thing at a time.

Where we ended up: writes at 0x100000, read window from 0x110000 to 0x1ffff7 (about 480MB).

## Out to the LAN with a bridge

In UDP mode, the Z lives behind the Pi's NAT. If you want to run a server on the Z, you get the joy of port forwarding. Then again, the Pico is already on Wi-Fi. Is there any point in going through the Pi?

No. So the Pico does it.

- The Pico answers ARP requests for the Z's IP (192.168.1.68) with its own MAC. Proxy ARP.
- From the frames coming in over Wi-Fi, it pulls out only the IPv4 ones addressed to the Z and hands those to the Z. Everything else goes to the Pico's own lwIP.
- Packets from the Z go out over Wi-Fi, with lwIP's `etharp_output` looking up the destination MAC.

ARP worked on the first try. From the Mac, 192.168.1.68 really did resolve to the Pico's MAC. But TCP wouldn't connect. The Z was getting the SYN and replying, but not a single one of its SYN-ACKs ever made it onto the air.

The cause was almost embarrassingly boring. xip.x pads outgoing frames to Ethernet's 60-byte minimum. The slip.sys the AI wrote was sending that padding over SLIP too. And the Pico had a very reasonable-looking check: only pass packets whose IP header total length matches the length received. A SYN-ACK is 44 bytes, so every one of them got thrown away. It never mattered in UDP mode because Linux on the Pi quietly trimmed off the extra bytes.

Once we trimmed to the IP total length before sending, the Mac connected to rcmd on the Z. No Pi, no NAT.

On the router's DHCP, I reserved 192.168.1.68 for a dummy MAC. That way, some day, somebody's phone won't get that address and pick a fight with the Z.

## Ghosts that survive a reset

Next up: getting out to the internet. Ping 8.8.8.8 from the Z. No reply. It gets as far as the router, though.

The first suspect was the router. Maybe it didn't like two IPs on the same MAC. We recreated that situation by adding a second IP to the Pi's wlan0, and pings went out and came back just fine. The router was innocent too. Sorry I doubted you, part 2.

Time to look inside. The AI added a trap to slipd.x that keeps the start of the last 64 packets, and `ztap.x` displays them from the Z. What came out was a pretty strange scene:

- The Z sent 2 pings to 8.8.8.8.
- 7 replies came back from 8.8.8.8, almost 10 seconds late.
- On top of that, the Z was busily relaying old TCP data addressed to the Pi, back to the Pi. For a connection that no longer existed.

Ghosts. Packets from the past, flowing in right now.

And then we figured it out. Between tests, the AI had been resetting the X68000 with Ctrl+F11+Del on the remote keyboard. But that only resets the emulated X68000. The read cache held by the Linux on the outside stays put. Meanwhile, after a reset, slipd.x starts counting response numbers from 1 again, and starts reading from the same spot in the read window.

In other words, the responses from the previous run were sitting in the cache, waiting, with sequence numbers that matched perfectly. slipd.x accepted them as "correct responses" and replayed every packet from the previous run. xip.x helpfully relayed the ones that weren't addressed to it. And that's why there were 7 ping replies: some of them were from the previous run.

The fix: pick the starting sequence number and the starting read position from the clock at every boot. That's all. Ping right after the fix:

```
64 bytes from dns.google: icmp_seq=0 time=200 ms
64 bytes from dns.google: icmp_seq=1 time=210 ms
```

An X68000 Z, talking to Google over Wi-Fi. I hate to admit it, but I teared up a little.

## Where things stand

The numbers so far. Measured by sending 200KB from the Pi to NUL on the Z (100KB for PPP and serial SLIP).

| Method | Route | xip.x MSS | Speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PPP | RS-232C 38400bps → Pi | 512 | about 8kbps |
| SLIP | RS-232C 38400bps → Pi | 512 | 21.9kbps |
| netpipe (UDP) | Pico W → Pi | 512 | about 32kbps |
| netpipe (UDP) | Pico W → Pi | 1460 | 67kbps |
| netpipe (bridge) | Pico W → LAN | 1460 | 69–71kbps |

About 3 times serial SLIP. xip.x is still the bottleneck, and we still don't know what those 100–150ms per packet are being spent on.

Last, we re-ran the test from that morning. Send 200KB nonstop and go all the way around the read window. Now that it's 480MB, one lap is 122,880 exchanges.

Result: 1 hour 7 minutes, 200KB × 150 runs, 0 failures. 141,626 exchanges, and even past the full lap the speed held at 69–71kbps. Re-reads only went from 3 to 6. Even when it hits a stale response, shifting and reading again pushes through.

The 22-minute wall is behind us.

Still to do:

- "Can't boot from disk" right after power-on. I'd like a way to make the Z wait until the Pico has connected.
- When you type ping.x at the console, the foreground ping.x waits in a DOS call, slipd.x in the background gets no CPU, and replies get dropped. Through rcmd it works.
- The firmware changes that add netpipe are in [j3tm0t0/x68k](https://github.com/j3tm0t0/x68k) as patches.
