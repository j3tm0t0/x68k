All right. Let's put SLIP on SCSI.

## netpipe

In x68kzremotedrv, SCSI ID 6 is a "communication disk" used for drive B: traffic. The AI's plan: borrow its unused LBAs and carry SLIP bytes through them. Write to a disk, read from a disk, and call it networking. We named it netpipe.

![The route: UDP mode this time; the bridge comes in part 5]({{ "/assets/netpipe.svg" | relative_url }})

1. On the Z, `slipd.x -z` writes the bytes it wants to send to LBA 0x100000, along with a 16-byte header (`X6NP`, a sequence number, a length).
2. When the Pico gets the write, it sends the contents to the Pi over UDP, and builds a response out of whatever has arrived from the Pi so far.
3. slipd.x reads the response, and if the sequence number matches, it hands the received data to xip.x.
4. While traffic keeps flowing, it does the next exchange right away. When things are quiet, it polls every 20–50ms.

On the Pi side, the AI wrote `slipudp.py`, which connects UDP to a pseudo-terminal, and slattach turns that into sl0. Same setup as SLIP over serial, just without the cable.

One thing the AI was worried about: the Z itself (inside, it's an emulator running on Linux) caches SCSI reads. Read the same LBA twice and the request never reaches the Pico. You just get the old contents back. So responses are read from a fixed range (call it the read window), shifting 8 sectors further each time, and wrapping back to the start when it hits the end. The B: driver (scsiremote.sys) did the same thing, so we copied it.

That cache is going to make my life miserable later. Consider yourself warned.

## It connects. It's slow

The Pi connected to rcmd on the Z, through SCSI and Wi-Fi. I'll admit I was a little moved.

The AI measured the speed. About 32kbps. 1.5 times serial.

…Okay.

First, the AI suspected the Pico.

- Wi-Fi power saving was on, and it was delaying responses by 50–300ms. Turning it off brought the round trip to the Pi from 48ms down to 10ms.
- One SCSI read or write takes 0.2–2.5ms. Not the bottleneck either.

Still no change. By process of elimination, what's left is xip.x on the Z. Line up the packets going in and out with timestamps, and xip.x was taking 100–150ms per packet, regardless of packet size.

We went through the usual tuning knobs, too.

- The window (`-w`), the speed-up factor (`-n`), Human68k's time slice (`PROCESS`). None of them helped.
- At `-w 16384` or higher, xip.x runs out of heap and freezes. The AI tried to raise the heap by writing `-h:1048576`, which got parsed as some other weird option, and the Z went down with an illegal instruction. Guess who pressed the reset button. The correct form, apparently, is `-+-h:bytes`. Who would ever guess that?

## Changing 2 bytes of a binary

If each packet takes a fixed amount of time, the only option is to carry more per packet. But xip.x hardcodes the TCP MSS at 512, and there's no setting to change it.

"Let's disassemble xip.x," I said. Easier said than done.

The AI picked xip.x apart on XEiJ. The code that builds the TCP MSS option and the code that sets the sender's initial value both read the same word in the data section (`$0200`). Change it to `$05B4` (1460).

2 bytes. Just 2 bytes.

| Method | xip.x MSS | Speed (Pi → Z, 200KB) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SLIP (RS-232C 38400bps) | 512 | 21.9kbps |
| netpipe (UDP) | 512 | about 32kbps |
| netpipe (UDP) | 1460 | 67kbps |

The time per packet stayed the same. Each packet now carries 3 times as much, and the speed went up to match. We also tried a patch that makes the receive and timer threads run in supervisor mode, but couldn't confirm any effect.

xip.x is only distributed as a binary, so I don't distribute the patched one. When the image is built, `patchxip.py` checks the SHA-256 of the original xip.x and then patches it.
