[Previously]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url en/2026-10-04-psp-diary-2 %}): The heavy part wasn't the 68000, it was the VRAM front desk. You can't read the PSP's CPU by counting instructions. Chō Renshā 68K got 3.3 times faster than before the improvements, and XVI mode became selectable. Code and measurements, once again, are almost all the AI (Claude).

## The sound lags

Up to now, all I'd looked at was FPS numbers. Today I use my ears.

"Try playing on the PSP-3000 again, with sound on. I feel like the sound is running a bit behind."

The AI looked into it. The sound was lagging, and it was stretched. Here's how:

- The main loop advanced frames by looking only at a millisecond timer. So the emulation's clock and the audio output's clock slowly drifted apart.
- When the sound ran short, the audio callback filled the gap by synthesizing FM sound on its own. That pushed the sound chip ahead of the emulation, and the music got stretched out.
- And that synthesis stole CPU time, so the emulation fell even further behind. The heavier the scene, the deeper it sank into this loop.
- On the flip side, whenever the emulation got ahead, the surplus piled up as latency.

A vicious cycle. The fix was to flip the thinking: **make the audio output the clock.** When the synthesized sound queued up drops below 35ms, run the next frame. Not a timer. The speaker sets the pace of the emulation.

Result: the sound no longer cuts out during play. Latency dropped by about half. We also shrank the output buffer from 2048 samples to 512 (46ms to 11.6ms).

## Measuring crackling sound

"In XVI mode with full frame, it drops below 40fps, and the sound is kind of crackly and hard to listen to."

How do you judge whether sound is crackling? My ears are not a measuring instrument.

"Could you judge it if I ran audio in from the PSP?" I asked. Then I realized it myself. "If you record to the Memory Stick, it'll drop frames."

The AI's answer was `arec`, which records to RAM and writes it out afterwards. For each callback, it logs the time, how much sound was ready, and how much the callback had to synthesize itself, to a CSV.

What we learned: when sound synthesized at 11025Hz or 22050Hz was output at 44.1kHz, each sample was being repeated 4 or 2 times. A staircase waveform sounds "crackly" to the ear. We switched to linear interpolation, using a moving average over as many samples as get repeated. Not a single division. For 2 and 4, shifts are enough.

Also, the buffer I'd just shrunk to 512 went back up to 1024 samples (23ms). Shrink it too far and the sound starts cutting out again.

## Defining the goal

"Maybe this is about as far as it goes?"

Right after whining, I redefined the goal myself. "I think it's done when Full Frame runs without any slowdown."

Mission definition: **draw every frame, sound on, no slowdown.**

## Measuring makes it slower

To know where the time goes, you need a profiler. px68k had a mechanism, `prof on`, that times each section.

We measured on the real hardware. With the profiler on, frame time more than doubled.

The measuring instrument was heavier than the thing being measured. It grabbed the time with a system call at every section boundary, which meant thousands of calls per frame.

The AI rebuilt it as a sampling profiler. The code just writes one byte saying "here's what I'm doing right now." A timer interrupt peeks at that byte at regular intervals (deliberately jittered, so it doesn't sync up with the line period) and counts. Peeking costs something too, so the interval is 1ms, which keeps the overhead to a few percent.

The first thing the new profiler found wasn't the emulator. It was the debug server's thread. While Wi-Fi was off, `select()` failed immediately, and it just kept retrying, over and over. It had a higher priority than the emulator, so it was eating a big chunk of every frame. The first fix didn't work. The second one, after midnight, finally did: while Wi-Fi is down, don't touch the socket at all.

## A PSP in handcuffs

In the evening I was playing it myself, and it felt heavy somehow.

"Ahh, maybe I was testing at 222."

Right. If the debug key file is present, px68k connects to Wi-Fi at startup. While it's connected, the PSP runs at 222MHz. I'd been playing on a PSP in handcuffs the whole time.

"Maybe we should be able to turn debug off from the menu." So now there's a Debug Net menu. Off drops Wi-Fi and goes back to 333MHz.

## The compiler's job

The last change of the day, just before midnight. The most boring one, and the one that helped most. Compiler options.

`-G8` is an option that lets global variables of 8 bytes or less be read in a single instruction. PGO (profile-guided optimization) is a system where the compiler looks at how often things actually run and decides where to put the code. We added both and measured on the real hardware, and the same scene got clearly faster.

As I said last time, on the PSP, instruction layout decides speed. The 68000 core and the per-slice and per-line work all fight over the 16KB instruction cache, many times per frame. PGO puts the well-traveled paths together.

We train the profile on PPSSPP. Execution counts don't depend on speed, so running from boot to the demo screen on the emulator is enough. The resulting `.gcda` files are checked into the repository, so if you just want to build, you don't need to retrain.

Not one line of code changed, and look at that. The one who did the best work today was the compiler.
