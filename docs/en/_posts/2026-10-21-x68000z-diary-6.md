The bottleneck is xip.x, at 100–150ms per packet. Spent on what, nobody knows. In this series, the AI (Claude) does almost all of the code and research.

## Easier said than done

"If we want it any faster, it's xip.x, right? Let's figure out what those 100–150ms are and speed it up. Or just rewrite it from scratch," I said. Easier said than done.

The AI didn't jump straight into a rewrite. First we measure, it said. Correct. Rewriting something when you don't know where its time goes is like cleaning a room blindfolded.

## A profiler on the Z

There's no profiler for the X68000. So we build one.

The idea is simple. Hook the vertical-sync interrupt (about 55 times a second) in slipd.x, and record the address the CPU was executing at the moment it got interrupted. Collect 4,096 of those, and dump them to a file with `zprof.x`. On the Mac, sort each address into a bucket: somewhere in xip.x, the SCSI ROM, Human68k, or slipd.x. To find where xip.x sits in memory, we searched for the `TCP/IP` signature at the start of its text section.

Here's what we got, recorded while pushing 200KB through:

- The CPU never rests, not even for a second. The 68000 is saturated.
- Two thirds of that is the SCSI ROM and slipd.x. In other words, netpipe's "hauling."
- xip.x's share: 16%.

xip.x wasn't slow. It just wasn't getting any CPU. Sorry I doubted you, part 3.

## Why the hauling is so heavy

Issue one SCSI command, and the ROM spins in a loop waiting until the USB round trip with the Pico is done. A few milliseconds. The CPU does nothing during that time, and nobody else gets it either.

If that were all, fine, nothing to be done. The problem was how many times it happened. Right after any traffic, slipd.x kept doing 20 empty exchanges in a row, just in case more was coming. That's 6–7 exchanges with nothing in them for every packet. The AI wrote that. Not me.

On top of that, it was unwrapping the received SLIP by calling a function for every single byte. On a 68000, function calls aren't cheap.

## Fixing slipd.x

Here's what the AI did:

- Instead of unwrapping SLIP one byte at a time, it copies whole runs of ordinary bytes at once.
- No more strings of empty exchanges. It only goes straight back for more when the Pico's response says "there's more."
- While data is flowing, it reads 8 sectors with a single command. When things are quiet, just 1 sector.
- The polling interval when quiet is now 60ms (changeable with `-i`).

Result:

| | Speed (Pi → Z, 200KB) | ping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before | 70kbps | 90–150ms |
| After | 136kbps | 90–150ms |
| After, interval 100ms | about 144kbps | about 170ms |

Twice as fast. Without touching a single byte of xip.x.

## What didn't work

For the record, we did try the xip.x side too.

- Human68k's time slice from 50 down to 20. No change.
- xip.x's window at 16KB. No change. Seen from the Pi: 1.6-second round trips, with 9–12 segments sent and still waiting for an ack. The clog is inside the Z.
- xip.x's timer thread was sweeping five tables every time the clock advanced, even by 1/100 of a second. A patch that makes it sweep every 0.1 seconds instead (`patchxip.py --timer`) gives 136–153kbps. Faster, but close to the noise. Not on by default.

## The ghosts are back

The first time we tried `--timer`, the Z wouldn't connect at all. The AI and I both assumed the patch had broken it.

Nope. After dozens of resets for testing, the Z's read cache had built up over 100MB of the read window. Every time slipd.x hit a stale response, it shifted 8 sectors and read again, but the next spot was cached too. 475 exchanges, 1,681 re-reads, 419 errors. A close relative of last time's ghosts.

New rule: on a stale response, jump 16MB ahead, up to 64 times. That fixed it. `--timer` was innocent. Sorry I doubted you, part 4. I should really start measuring before I start doubting.

## Where things stand

The profile after the fix: xip.x 28%, SCSI ROM 25%, slipd.x 15%, Human68k 10%, and the rest is rcmd and friends. xip.x's share went up. Inside xip.x, the hot spots are a loop spinning while fighting over a lock, and the routing table sweep.

To shave off any more, we'd have to rewrite parts of the xip.x binary much more deeply, or actually rewrite the whole thing. Easier said than done.
