Full disclosure, same as the X68000 Z diary: the code in this series, the measurements, and even this write-up were mostly done by an AI (Claude). My job is to turn the PSP on, turn the PSP back on after it crashes, and listen to the sound and say "that's weird."

So what happened? I spent five days tuning the PSP port of px68k, an X68000 emulator. Games that used to run like a slideshow got to the point where you can play them at full speed. I'll tell the story in five parts. This is where it starts.

![A PSP Go running Chō Renshā 68K. The 53.8 in the top left is the FPS counter]({{ "/assets/psp-go-chourensha.jpg" | relative_url }})

This is the work site. Chō Renshā 68K running on a PSP Go. The 53.8 in the top left is the FPS counter. I took this photo at the end of day five. The rest of this series is how I got there.

## Status report

- I have two PSPs. A PSP-3000 and a PSP Go. Both run CFW, both have 64MB of memory.
- px68k, the X68000 emulator hissorii wrote in 2013, has a PSP port. I forked it ([j3tm0t0/px68k](https://github.com/j3tm0t0/px68k)).
- Goal: run X68000 games on the PSP at full speed.

What about ROMs? For the IPL ROM, I use the one Sharp once released for free. The CG ROM (the character font) was never released, so I had the AI build one out of free bitmap fonts like Shinonome. For games, I only use ones their authors give away for free.

My first instructions to the AI: "Install pspdev and get it building. Measure FPS on the real hardware. Then tune, in this order: FM sound onto the Media Engine, video compositing, the 68000 core."

I even gave it the order. That order lasted about an hour. That's how these things go.

## It slows down when you watch it

It built. After fixing the five places GCC 15 yelled about, Human68k booted on the PSP.

We learned something along the way. While a PSP is connected to Wi-Fi, its CPU is locked at 222MHz. That's how the firmware works, and CFW can't raise it. The AI had been pulling debug logs over Wi-Fi, so some of the slowness we were seeing was just that. Result: a `bench` command that turns Wi-Fi off and goes back to 333MHz, only while measuring.

Note to self: this PSP slows down when you watch it.

## An emulator on an emulator

"Run several optimizations in parallel. You, the main one, are in charge of testing."

The AI split up the work. A video team, a CPU team, and a test-disk team. The main AI became the guardian of regression tests. Its job: use PPSSPP (a PSP emulator) to capture the screen at a fixed frame, and confirm that not a single pixel changed between before and after an optimization. Running an emulator on top of an emulator and comparing screenshots. It's emulators all the way down.

The test-disk team gathered freely available software and built 12 floppies that boot automatically from the freely released Human68k 3.02. Chō Renshā 68K, Otoko Dama, SION II and IV, the XSP samples, an image viewer, and so on. We couldn't find any free software that used translucency, so we wrote a translucency test program ourselves (by "we" I mean the AI).

## Let the GPU draw it

"Still working on the GE optimization?"

The GE is the PSP's Graphics Engine. In other words, the GPU. All of the X68000's screen compositing (stacking the text, graphics, BG and sprite layers) was being done on the CPU. The plan: have the GE draw it. Keep a copy of VRAM in a form the GE can read, and hand compositing over to it.

The problem: the moment the 68000 writes to VRAM, the GE might still be reading the old contents. So every write to VRAM now goes through a function that tracks the generation of the GE's copy. Write through the back door even once, and the screen breaks.

We also found a bug from 2013 while we were in there. When VLINE was -1, the code wrote to `TextDirtyLine[-1]`, the slot just before the start of the array. For 13 years it had been quietly trashing the variable next door.

## Where does 55 come from?

I had the AI add an FPS display, and while I was staring at it, something bugged me.

"Where does 55 come from, again?"

The AI's answer: in the X68000's 31kHz screen modes, vertical sync is about **55.46Hz**. That's 18.03ms per frame. Inside px68k it's stored as `VSYNC_HIGH 180310` (in units of 0.1µs). For 15kHz games, full speed is 61.5Hz.

Huh. The target was never 60fps. It was 55.46fps. The PSP's LCD runs at 60Hz, so every so often the same image shows up twice. That's by design.

The budget is 18.03ms per frame. Not a catchy number, but I'm going to be writing it a lot.

"Drop the 'FPS' text. And the denominator." The numbers-only display in the photo at the top is the result.

## It won't boot

Around midnight, we hit the first wall. Not speed. Chō Renshā 68K wouldn't boot. It just stopped at the Human68k prompt.

The cause was SRAM. Without an `sram.dat`, px68k boots as an X68000 with 1MB of memory. Chō Renshā 68K needs 2MB or more. The game was silently refusing to run on an X68000 without enough memory.

Fix: when there's no `sram.dat`, build SRAM from the IPL ROM's defaults and the actual amount of memory.

Later, we took the unmodified px68k, added only the SRAM fix, and measured. Chō Renshā 68K took about **46ms** per frame. About 22fps. Faster than a slideshow, but that's not a shooter. That's our starting point.

## The 256-color wall

"Chō Renshā looks pretty heavy."

Now that it booted, Chō Renshā 68K was taking 30.5ms per frame, even with GE compositing. The reason was simple. The first version of GE compositing only handled 16-color mode. Chō Renshā 68K uses 256-color mode. So it got zero benefit from the GE, and the CPU was drawing everything.

We added 256-color support, and 30.5ms became **15.3ms**. Inside the 18.03ms budget.

## The next mountain

Before bed, I tried SION IV too.

"SION4 is really heavy too."

On the weapon select screen: 104–120ms per frame. About 10fps. Six times over budget.

## Next time

Faced with SION IV, the AI starts writing up a design for a dynamic recompiler for the 68000. Estimate: 6–10 weeks. Before that, we decide to measure.
